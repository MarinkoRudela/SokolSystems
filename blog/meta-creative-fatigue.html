Creative fatigue on Meta ads: what Meta’s own data says, the signs, and when to refresh

What creative fatigue is, what Meta’s own research and Ads Manager labels say about it, the five signs to watch, and why refreshing on a schedule is the wrong rule.

By Marinko Rudela · 26 October 2026 · 4 min read

Every ad that works eventually stops working as well. The people it reaches have seen it too many times, so fewer of them click and fewer of them buy. That’s creative fatigue. It doesn’t mean the ad was bad. As Meta ads consultant Jon Loomer puts it, “Fatigue doesn’t mean your creative is bad; it means your audience has seen it too often.”

Here’s what Meta’s own numbers say about it, how Ads Manager flags it, the signs to watch for, and what to do when it shows up.

What Meta’s research says

Two figures from Meta’s own research come up again and again. Both were published by Meta staff, and both come from Meta’s internal data, so they can’t be checked independently.

Meta research on fatigue and creative variety
FindingSource Meta gives
A 45% average drop in the likelihood of conversion after four repeated exposures to an adRegression analysis by Analytics @ Meta, May 2023
Ad sets with diversified creative showed up to 32% better performance and 9% more incremental reachInternal test of two identical vs two different creatives across 2,703 ad sets, April 2021

Independent research points the same way. In a study of mobile ad campaigns, researchers at the Japanese ad company CyberAgent found that click-through rate fell steadily as fatigue built up, and that switching creatives when fatigue was high improved it modestly.

How Ads Manager flags it

  • Delivery column. Meta can show “Creative fatigue” when an ad’s cost per result is at least twice as high as it has been in the past, and “Creative limited” when it’s higher but less than double, according to Jon Loomer’s write-up of Meta’s definitions.
  • Creative diversity. A newer column (Columns, then Customize columns) rates how visually varied the images and videos in a campaign or ad set are: Low, Medium or High. Meta labels it “in development”.
  • Creative breakdown. Since July 2025, flexible-format ads can show cost per result for each image or video, so you can see which asset is wearing out.

Why it matters more now

In December 2024 Meta described Andromeda, the system that picks which ads to show each person. Meta said the number of ad creatives in its systems “is expected to grow significantly” with generative AI, and that Andromeda “delivers a more diverse set of ad candidates.” In July 2025 Meta also dropped its old advice of six creatives per ad set from its help pages, in favor of keeping creative varied.

The practical upshot: Meta increasingly uses the creative itself to find the right people. Running one ad for months, or several ads that look almost the same, gives it less to work with.

Five signs an ad is fatiguing

Adapted from Darkroom’s checklist of fatigue signals:

  1. Frequency climbs while reach stalls. The same people keep seeing it.
  2. Click-through rate falls while impressions hold. It’s still being shown, but fewer people respond.
  3. Cost per result drifts above the ad’s own past level. Compare the ad with itself, not with other ads.
  4. CPM rises with no change in your market. Older ads can get more expensive to deliver than fresh ones.
  5. Conversion rate drops while click-through rate holds. The people still clicking are less likely to buy.

One honest caveat: with Meta’s automation, a rising cost can also come from audience saturation, more competition or a change on Meta’s side. Check more than one signal before you blame the creative.

When to refresh

Not on a calendar. Loomer’s advice is blunt: “You don’t need to refresh your ads every few weeks,” and “A high frequency, by itself, is not problematic.” If an ad is still delivering at a good cost, leave it running.

When the signs show, add rather than replace. Keep the ad running and add new ones beside it. The best place to start is the ad that’s been winning, because it already tells you what your audience responds to:

  • New hooks: the same ad with a different opening line or first three seconds.
  • New visuals: the same message in a new setting, with a new person or a new background.
  • New formats: the same angle as a static, a carousel, a short video or a UGC-style clip.
  • New angles: the same product sold on price, a problem it solves, a result, or what other customers say.

Make the differences real. Meta’s diversity column and Loomer both point the same way: near-identical versions add little. Change one clear thing per version, so your results show what made the difference.

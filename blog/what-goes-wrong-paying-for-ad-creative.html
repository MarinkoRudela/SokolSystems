Why paid ad creative goes wrong: what 167 reviews and forum posts say

We read 167 public reviews and forum posts about ads and marketing help that didn’t pay off. Here’s what went wrong most often, what cost the most, and seven questions to ask before you pay anyone.

By Marinko Rudela · 19 October 2026 · 4 min read

Before rewriting our own website, we read what people say when ads and the help they paid for don’t work out. We collected 167 first-hand accounts from public reviews and forum posts, kept them in the authors’ own words, and tagged each one by what went wrong.

The most common problem was that the help didn’t deliver the work: 52 of the 167 accounts. The most expensive was paying every month for promises, with bills of up to £3,100 a month.

1. The help didn’t deliver the work

The most common story came from people who hired freelancers or used design marketplaces. The work arrived late, ignored the brief or never arrived.

“Not sure anyone read the detailed brief I provided.”

A customer of a design marketplace

“He said I would have a draft in 5-7 days. That came and went and he never provided a draft.”

A customer, about a freelance designer

Each failure was usually cheap on its own, between $19 and $750 where people said, but it cost weeks of waiting and an ad that never ran.

2. Paying every month for promises

The most expensive stories involved marketing companies billing monthly, sometimes under 6- or 12-month contracts. People mentioned paying $400, $900, $1,300 and £3,100 a month.

“They over promise and underperform.”

A customer of a local marketing company

“the ads they charged me for never ran”

A local business owner, about a marketing company

The complaint wasn’t only the price. It was paying every month without being able to see what the money produced, and finding it hard to leave.

3. Spent on ads, nothing back

This was the single most common complaint from online store owners running their own ads. Many described real traffic but no sales, which usually points to the offer, the price or the store rather than the ad. Better creative can help when an ad isn’t getting clicks. It can’t fix a page that doesn’t convert, so we counted most of these as only partly a creative problem.

What almost nobody says: “ad fatigue”

Only 5 of the 167 described an ad that worked and then stopped. People don’t usually notice an ad wearing out. They notice money going out and less coming back. The pattern shows up more clearly in ad data: when we checked 35 local businesses in Meta’s Ad Library, half had an ad running for over a year, often with nothing new beside it.

Seven questions to ask before you pay anyone for ad creative

  1. What exactly do I get, and by when? Ask for a delivery time per request, and whether that means a first draft or a finished file.
  2. Is there a contract, and how do I cancel? Look for auto-renewals and minimum terms.
  3. Who reads my brief, and who makes the work? Know whether it’s one person, a rotating pool or a contest.
  4. Do you start from what’s already working? Your best ad is the best brief you have.
  5. What happens if the work misses the brief? Ask how revisions work and whether they cost extra.
  6. Do you promise results? Nobody can honestly guarantee ad performance. If someone does, ask exactly how they measure it and what happens when they miss.
  7. Do I own everything you make? Get it in writing.

How we did this

  • Sources: merchant threads on the Shopify Community forum, posts on Hacker News, and 1 to 3 star reviews on Sitejabber of marketing companies, lead platforms, freelance marketplaces and design marketplaces. Collected in October 2026.
  • We kept each account as a short, exact quote with a link to its source, then tagged it by what went wrong, what set it off, any cost mentioned, what the person had tried, and the result they wanted.
  • Limits: this isn’t a random sample, and people who post reviews skew toward bad experiences. Some sites, including Reddit and Trustpilot, weren’t reachable for this research, and only one restaurant owner is in the set. We don’t name the companies reviewed.
